Case study · Whole-life cost, sensitivity and risk analysis

What will the Royal Navy's Type 31 frigates cost to own?

An independent whole-life cost model for the five Type 31 frigates, built in Excel from published UK data, calibrated against US, Canadian and Australian programmes, and stress-tested with sensitivity analysis and a 2,000-run Monte Carlo simulation.

The approach

1. InputsEvery number in one sheet, tagged Sourced, Derived, Benchmark or Assumption, with low, base and high values.
2. Cost modelYear-by-year costs 2019-2065 for each ship: build, crew and fuel, maintenance, indirect support, mid-life update, disposal.
3. BenchmarkingChecked against US Navy, US GAO, Canadian and Australian evidence.
4. SensitivityEach uncertain input moved to its low and high value to rank what drives the answer.
5. RiskMonte Carlo simulation with triangular distributions gives a P10-P90 confidence range.
Core equation: Whole-life cost = Acquisition × (1 + growth) + Ships × Life × (Operating + Maintenance) × (1 + Indirect uplift) + Ships × (Mid-life update + Disposal)

Owning costs more than buying

The £2.3bn purchase price is only 38% of the total. Crew, fuel, maintenance, in-service support, mid-life updates and disposal add £3.7bn over the ships' lives.

Discounted at the HM Treasury Green Book rate of 3.5%, future costs from 2026 are worth £3.1bn today.

Calibrating with international data

Published UK running costs cover only direct costs. On those alone, support would be 52% of whole-life cost, below every comparator (59-72%).

The US Navy's FFG-62 frigate estimate shows indirect and sustaining support adding about 62% on top of direct costs. Applying that ratio lifts the Type 31 to 62%, and its annual running cost to 5.8% of unit build cost, close to FFG-62's 5.7%.

Without this step, the estimate would have been £4.8bn, understating the true cost by about £1.3bn.

What drives the answer

Service life is the biggest driver, followed by the indirect support and maintenance assumptions. These are also the least certain inputs, so they are where better data would most improve the estimate.

Crew size and disposal matter little, because the Type 31's small crew is already fixed by its design.

How confident can we be?

Across 2,000 simulated futures, 72% cost more than the point estimate, because most risks push costs up rather than down. Budgeting at P80 means holding about £698m of contingency.

Recommendations

Hold contingency to P80. Plan for about £6.7bn rather than the £6.0bn point estimate.
Publish the support case. The unpublished through-life support business case would replace the least certain assumptions.
Decide service life early. It is the largest single driver of whole-life cost.
Protect the small crew. Lean crewing is a key saving over the Type 23 it replaces.

Limitations

UK running costs are estimated by analogy with the Type 23 because the Type 31 support business case is not public. Overseas programmes have different cost structures, so they are compared as ratios rather than absolute figures. The £2.2bn budget mixes price years. Every assumption is explained and rated in the Excel model's Assumptions_Log sheet.
